\section{Limits and Colimits}

So far we have seen multiple construction with a universal mapping property
(UMP). With them being defined differently, most of them still do look-alike
very similar. In this chapter we are going to explain why that is, and we are
going to combine them under one central notion, the \textbf{limit} of a
diagram.

Chapter 04 of the book proceeds in three stages, and it helps to keep the line
of thought in mind:
\begin{description}
  \item[Two preparatory notions:] \textit{Subobjects} generalize subsets to
    arbitrary categories. \textit{Pullbacks} generalize both intersections and
    inverse images, and they let us transfer subobjects along arrows.
  \item[The general theory:] We define diagrams, cones and limits, and prove
    the main theorem: every limit can be built from products and equalizers. We
    then ask which functors preserve the limits. Representable functors always
    do.
  \item[Colimits in practice:] We dualize, and study pushouts and colimits of
    sequences. Sequences give direct limits of groups, the cumulative hierarchy
    of set theory, and the least fixed points used in the semantics of
    programming languages.
\end{description}

With this rough overview of the chapter established, we will start by
introducing our preparatory notions:

\subsection{Subobjects}

A subobject generalizes the notion of a ''subset'' $U \subseteq X$ to any
category, by replacing subset inclusion with a monomorphism $m: M
\rightarrowtail X$. As every subset also occurs as an equalizer, and all
equalizers are monic, it is natural to regard monos as \textit{generalized
subsets}: a mono in \textbf{Groups} can be regarded as a subgroup, a mono in
\textbf{Top} as a subspace, and so on.

To visualize the subobject definition and properties further, let's establish
an example for the remainder of the subobject section. Lets suppose $X$ is a
set of four seats $\{a, b, c, d\}$. The subobject can here be thought of a way
of seating a group of people, so that no two share a seat.

\subsubsection{Definition Subobject}

A subobject of $X$ in a category $\mathbf{C}$ is a monomorphism 
$$
m: M \rightarrowtail X.
$$
The idea here is derived from the category of structured sets \textbf{G}, where
given a monomorphism $m: M \rightarrowtail X$ (in the book referred to as
''gadgets''), then the image $m(M) = \{m(y) \mid y \in M\} \subseteq X$ is often a
sub-gadget of $X$. To this sub-gadget, $M$ is isomorphic via $m$:
$$
m: M \overset{\sim}{\rightarrow} m(M) \subseteq X
$$

To clarify: $m$ carries the same information as the sub-gadget $m(M)$. To bring
this thought even further, one can think of the mono $m$ itself as determining
a ''part'' of $X$. This is now not bound to categories with functions as arrows
anymore and this is why our definition derives from it.

It is important to understand here, that $M$ in itself does not give any
information about which part of $X$ it is the subobject of. This comes solely
from the mono $m$. In our established example this would look something like
this:

\begin{tikzpicture}[every node/.style={circle, draw, inner sep=2pt, node distance=3mm}]
  \node[draw=none] (M) {$M$};
  \node[draw=none, right=1.5cm of M] (X1) {$X$};

  \node[below=3mm of M] (1) {$1$};
  \node[below=of 1]     (2) {$2$};

  \node[below=1mm of X1] (a1) {$a$};

Here both $M$ and $M'$ describe the same part of $X$, but coming from a two
different objects. Without any additional information it is going to become
difficult for us to compare these subobjects. We have passed from the preorder
to the poset.

\subsubsection{Category of subobjects}

\[
\begin{tikzcd}
  A \ar[rr, "f", shift left] \ar[dr, "m"'] & & M' \ar[dl, "m'"] \\
                                           & X &
\end{tikzcd}
\]

Given subobjects $m, m'$ of $X$ and an arrow $f: m \rightarrow m'$ in the slice
category $\mathbf{C} / X$, with $m' \circ f = m$. This gives the category
$\text{Sub}_{\mathbf{C}}(X)$ of subobjects of $X$ in $\mathbf{C}$.

This category is a preord: there is at most one $f$ as above, since $m'f = m =
m \tilde{f}$ implies $f = \tilde{f}$ because $m'$ is monic. We therefore define
the inclusion $m \subseteq m'$ to mean that such an $f$ exists.

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[every node/.style={circle, draw, inner sep=2pt, node distance=4mm}]
  \node[draw=none] at (0, 3.5) {$M$};
  \node[draw=none] at (6, 3.5) {$M'$};
  \node[draw=none] at (3.5, -0.5) {$X$};

  \node (m1) at (0, 3) {$1$};
  \node (m2) at (0, 2) {$2$};

  \node (mp1) at (6, 3) {$x$};
  \node (mp2) at (6, 2) {$y$};

  \node (a) at (2, 0) {$a$};
  \node (b) at (3, 0) {$b$};
  \node (c) at (4, 0) {$c$};
  \node (d) at (5, 0) {$d$};

  \draw[<->, dashed] (m1) -- (mp2);
  \draw[<->, dashed] (m2) -- (mp1);

  \draw[->] (m1) -- (a);
  \draw[->] (m2) -- (b);

  % Right mappings
  \draw[->] (mp1) -- (b);
  \draw[->] (mp2) -- (a);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

By looking at the above example we get an idea on how this might look on
objects $M, M'$ being both subobjects of $X$. Also in the diagram above there
exists $f: m \rightarrow m'$ and also an arrow $f': m' \rightarrow m$.
Therefore $m \subseteq m'$ and $m' \subseteq m$, meaning they are isomorphic as
subobject and we now can specify equivalence $m \equiv m'$ between subobjects
as well. We can think of this as $m$ and $m'$ determining the same part.

Bringing the idea of equivalent subobjects even further we can treat all
equivalent monos as one thing. Under this definition a subobject is an
equivalence class of monos under mutual inclusion.

From now on, both notions will be used and it is made clear when monos or
equivalence classes are intended.

\subsubsection{Subobjects of subobjects}

When $M' \subseteq M$ via a morphism $f$, then the arrow $f$ is itself a mono,
so $M'$ is also a subobject of $M$. An example for this would be $4\mathbb{Z}
\subseteq 2\mathbb{Z} \subseteq \mathbb{Z}$, where the set $4\mathbb{Z}$ is a
part of $2\mathbb{Z}$ which is again a part of $\mathbb{Z}$.

This shows that we have a functor $\text{Sub}(M) \rightarrow \text{Sub}(X)$
defined by the composition with $f$.

\subsubsection{Generalized elements}

Let $z: Z \rightarrow X$ be a generalized element of an object $X$. We can
define a \textit{local membership relation} $z \in_{X} M$ between such elements
$z$ and subobjects $m: M \rightarrowtail X$ by $z \in_{X} M$ iff there exists
$f: Z \rightarrow M$ such that $z = mf$.

We can think of $z$ like being a probe, where all elements of it need to land
in $X$. In the above we can see that on the left all elements of $Z$ land in $M
\subseteq X$ but on the right one of the elements lands outside, meaning for
the left $x \in_{X} M$ and on the right $x \not\in_{X} M$. We do this so
subobjects behave like subsets (\exref{5.4}.)

\subsubsection{Equalizers as subobjects}

\[
\begin{tikzcd}
  E \ar[r] & A \ar[r, "f", shift left] \ar[r, "g"', shift right] & B
\end{tikzcd}
\]

We can now write equalizers as being a subobject of $A$ with property $z
\in_{A} E$ iff $f(z) = g(z)$, which can be brought further by regarding $E$ as
the subobject of generalized elements $z: Z \rightarrow A$ such that $f(z) =
g(z)$:
$$
E = \{ z \in A \mid f(z) = g(z) \} \subseteq A.
$$

\subsection{Pullbacks}

We can think of pullbacks as the collection of all pairs that agree on
something. We are going to establish another example for this section to help
you understand this better. Let $A$ be a set of students, $B$ be a set of
restaurants, and $C$ be a set of cities. Each student lives in a city, and each
restaurant is in a city. We can think of a pullback as every (student,
restaurant) pair in the same city. To anyone familiar with SQL and relational
algebra this will sound familiar, as it describes exactly an inner join on the
city.

\subsubsection{Definition of a pullback}

\[
\begin{tikzcd}
  P \ar[d, "p_{1}"'] \ar[r, "p_{2}"] & B \ar[d, "g"] \\
  A \ar[r, "f"'] & C
\end{tikzcd}
\]

We start with two arrows $f: A \rightarrow C$ and $g: B \rightarrow C$ into the
same object, which we call a corner. The pullback of $f$ and $g$ consists of
arrows $p_{1}: P \rightarrow A$ and $p_{2}: P \rightarrow B$ with $fp_{1} =
gp_{2}$, such that for any $z_{1}: Z \rightarrow A$ and $z_{2}: Z \rightarrow B$
with $fz_{1} = gz_{2}$ there exists a unique $u: Z \rightarrow P$ with $z_{1} =
p_{1}u$ and $z_{2} = p_{2}u$. Pullbacks are unique up to isomorphism, as they
are given by a UMP.

To bring this back to our example: for any pair in $P$, the student's city and
the restaurant's city are the same.

We can also describe this using product-style notation, writing $P = A \times_{C}
B$. A generalized element $z$ of $A \times_{C} B$ is a pair $\langle z_{1}, z_{2}
\rangle$ with $fz_{1} = gz_{2}$. This yields $A \times_{C} B = \{ \langle z_{1},
z_{2} \rangle \in A \times B \mid fz_{1} = gz_{2} \}$, which as a subobject of
$A \times B$ is the equalizer of $f \circ \pi_{1}$ and $g \circ \pi_{2}$.

As Proposition 5.5 in the book already states, this is valid for all categories
with products and equalizers. We can take a simple SQL statement to illustrate
this:
\begin{verbatim}
SELECT *                     -- start from a product A x B
FROM student s, restaurant r
WHERE s.city = r.city        -- equalize f . pi_{1} and
                             --          g . pi_{2}
\end{verbatim}
With our definition ($A$ being the students, $B$ being the restaurants) and $A
\times B$ there are now 2 maps to $C$ (being the cities): $f \pi_{1}$ is the
student's city, $g \pi_{2}$ is the restaurants city. The equalizer $e: E
\rightarrowtail A \times B$ is the part where these 2 agree, which is the
pullback. The pullbacks projections are $p_{1} = \pi_{1} e$ and $p_{2} =
\pi_{2} e$.

\subsubsection{Special cases}

\paragraph{Products.} If $C = 1$ is terminal, then $fz_{1} = gz_{2}$ always
holds, since there is only one arrow $Z \rightarrow 1$. The condition
disappears, and the pullback is just the product: $A \times_{1} B \cong A \times
B$.

\paragraph{Inverse images.} Let $V \subseteq C$ be the cities with a
university, with inclusion $i: V \rightarrowtail C$. Pulling back $i$ along $f$
gives the square
\[
\begin{tikzcd}
  f^{-1}(V) \ar[r, "\bar{f}"] \ar[d, tail, "j"'] & V \ar[d, tail, "i"] \\
  A \ar[r, "f"'] & C
\end{tikzcd}
\]
where $j$ is the inclusion and $\bar{f}$ is $f$ restricted to $f^{-1}(V)$. As
the square commutes, every student in $f^{-1}(V)$ lives in a city of $V$. The
UMP makes sure that every such student is included. Together this gives
\[
  f^{-1}(V) = \{ a \in A \mid f(a) \in V \}.
\]
This description only uses arrows, so we can use it in any category: the
inverse image of a subobject $m: M \rightarrowtail C$ along $f$ is defined as
its pullback. The pullback of a mono is again a mono (\exref{5.3}), so this
is a subobject of $A$, and we get
\[
  f^{-1}: \text{Sub}(C) \rightarrow \text{Sub}(A).
\]

\paragraph{Intersections.} If both arrows are inclusions $U, V \subseteq X$,
then a pair $(u, v)$ matches exactly when $u = v$, so the pullback is $U \cap
V$.

\subsubsection{Two-pullback lemma}

\[
\begin{tikzcd}
  F \ar[r, "f'"] \ar[d, "h''"'] & E \ar[r, "g'"] \ar[d, "h'"] & D \ar[d, "h"] \\
  A \ar[r, "f"'] & B \ar[r, "g"'] & C
\end{tikzcd}
\]
We consider the commutative diagram above in a category with pullbacks. The
lemma states that
\begin{enumerate}
  \item if the two squares ($E, B, C, D$ and $F, A, B, E$) are pullbacks, the
    outer rectangle ($F, A, C, D$) is a pullback as well:
    $$
    A \times_{B} (B \times_{C} D) \cong A \times_{C} D
    $$
  \item if the right square, and the outer rectangle are pullbacks, the left
    square is a pullback as well.
\end{enumerate}

Both proofs use one trick: an arrow into a pullback is determined by its two
components, by the uniqueness in the UMP.

\textit{Proof of 1.} Let $z_{1}: Z \rightarrow A$ and $z_{2}: Z \rightarrow D$
with $gfz_{1} = hz_{2}$. We pass this through one square at a time. The pair
$(fz_{1}, z_{2})$ fits the right square, so there is a unique $v: Z
\rightarrow E$ with $h'v = fz_{1}$ and $g'v = z_{2}$. Now the pair $(z_{1}, v)$
fits the left square, so there is a unique $u: Z \rightarrow F$ with $h''u =
z_{1}$ and $f'u = v$, and thus $g'f'u = z_{2}$. For uniqueness, let $u'$ also
satisfy $h''u' = z_{1}$ and $g'f'u' = z_{2}$. Then $h'f'u' = fh''u' = fz_{1}$,
so $f'u'$ has the same components as $v$ and $f'u' = v$. Hence $u'$ has the
same components as $u$, and $u' = u$.

\textit{Proof of 2.} Let $z_{1}: Z \rightarrow A$ and $v: Z \rightarrow E$ with
$fz_{1} = h'v$. Then $gfz_{1} = gh'v = hg'v$, so the outer rectangle gives a
unique $u$ with $h''u = z_{1}$ and $g'f'u = g'v$. It remains to show $f'u = v$.
Both are arrows into the right pullback, and they have the same components:
$g'f'u = g'v$, and $h'f'u = fh''u = fz_{1} = h'v$. So $f'u = v$. For
uniqueness, any $u'$ with $h''u' = z_{1}$ and $f'u' = v$ also satisfies $g'f'u'
= g'v$, so $u' = u$ by the outer rectangle.

We can apply our example to this again. $A$ is still the students, $B$ is
cities, $C$ is countries, and $D$ is consulates, each in a country. In the
right square $E$ pairs a city with a consulate in the same country. In the left
square $F$ pairs a student with one of those (city, consulate) ''rows'',
matching on the student's city. The outer rectangle then pairs a student with a
consulate in the student's county.

\discussion{Corollary 5.9}

Let $\alpha = \beta\gamma$ be a commutative triangle over $C$, and let $h: C'
\rightarrow C$. Pull back $\alpha$ and $\beta$ along $h$, giving $\alpha'$ and
$\beta'$. Then there is a unique $\gamma'$ making the diagram below commute,
and the upper square is again a pullback.
\[
\begin{tikzcd}
  A' \ar[r, "h_{\alpha}"] \ar[d, dashed, "\gamma'"'] & A \ar[d, "\gamma"] \\
  B' \ar[r, "h_{\beta}"] \ar[d, "\beta'"'] & B \ar[d, "\beta"] \\
  C' \ar[r, "h"'] & C
\end{tikzcd}
\]

\textit{Proof.} The pair $(\gamma h_{\alpha}, \alpha')$ fits the lower
pullback, since $\beta\gamma h_{\alpha} = \alpha h_{\alpha} = h\alpha'$. So
there is a unique $\gamma': A' \rightarrow B'$ with $h_{\beta}\gamma' = \gamma
h_{\alpha}$ and $\beta'\gamma' = \alpha'$. Now the outer rectangle has sides
$\beta\gamma = \alpha$ and $\beta'\gamma' = \alpha'$, so it is exactly the
pullback of $\alpha$ along $h$. The lower square is a pullback as well. Turning
the diagram by $90^{\circ}$, part 2 of the two-pullback lemma (with the lower
square as the right one) shows that the upper square is a pullback.

So pulling back along $h$ sends an arrow $\gamma: \alpha \rightarrow \beta$ of
$\mathbf{C}/C$ to an arrow $\gamma': \alpha' \rightarrow \beta'$ of
$\mathbf{C}/C'$. This is what makes pullback a functor (Proposition 5.10).
